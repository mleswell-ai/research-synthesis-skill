# Synthesising primary qualitative data

For interviews, focus groups, open survey responses, feedback, support tickets, call
notes and observation notes. The approach follows thematic analysis as practised in
qualitative research, with the ledger keeping themes tied to the data.

## Putting the data in the ledger

- Each participant, session or record is a source with `type: "primary-data"`. Put the
  attributes that matter to the analysis (role, tenure, site, segment) in `context`.
- Each participant is their own `lineage`. Two people who were interviewed together, or
  one person interviewed twice, share one.
- Appraise each source for this question: was this person placed to know? Were they
  describing their own experience or someone else's? Was the answer prompted?
- Record in `protocol` how participants were recruited and what they were asked. Both
  limit what the data can show.
- Locators are line ranges, timestamps or question numbers.

The ceiling rules then do useful work unprompted: a theme resting on one participant
cannot be rated above moderate, and one resting only on second-hand accounts cannot be
rated above low.

## What each kind of material can show

| Material | Good for | Blind spot |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Interviews | Reasons, sequence, context | Few people; shaped by the questions |
| Focus groups | Range of views; how people respond to each other | Louder voices; agreement for its own sake |
| Open survey answers | Breadth | Brief; no follow-up; only those who chose to answer |
| Tickets and complaints | Real problems at volume | Silent about what works and who did not complain |
| Call or meeting notes | Priorities and objections | Already filtered by the note-taker |
| Observation | What people do | The observer's reading of it |

Where two kinds are available, use one to test the other.

## Six passes through the data

Each pass has a product. Do them in order.

| Pass | Do | Product |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 Read | Read everything once before labelling anything. Note what was asked | A sense of the whole; first impressions written down so they can be tested later |
| 2 Code | Label passages with short codes that stay close to what was said or done | Evidence items in the ledger, each with a locator |
| 3 Gather | Bring together codes that describe one underlying thing across participants | Candidate themes |
| 4 Test | Go back through the full data set with each candidate in hand (next section) | Themes that survived, were split, merged or dropped |
| 5 State | Write each surviving theme as a claim, with its conditions | Claims in the ledger with support and contradicting items |
| 6 Weigh | Run `ceilings`; rate; work out what follows for the decision | Rated findings, then interpretations and recommendations based on them |

In pass 2, let the codes come from the data unless the study was designed around a
framework. Many codes at this stage is normal. In pass 3, group by meaning: participants
use different words for one thing and the same word for different things.

Pass 4 is the one most often skipped, and skipping it is how a first impression becomes
a finding.

## Testing a theme

Take each candidate back to the data and ask:

- **Who says it?** Several participants, or one participant several times?
- **Was it offered or elicited?** Agreement with a leading question is weak evidence.
- **Is it specific?** Incidents, examples and described behaviour count for more than
  assent to a general statement.
- **Who does not fit?** Find them, and work out why (next sections).
- **Does it hold across groups?** If only one site, role or cohort shows it, the theme
  is about that group.
- **Is it one thing?** If the supporting passages pull in two directions, split it.
- **Would the first impression from pass 1 have predicted it?** If every theme matches
  what was expected before coding, look harder at what was not expected.

A single vivid remark is a quotation. If it matters, report it as one person's view.

State what survives as a claim, not a label:

| Label | Claim |
| --- | --- |
| "Communication" | "Staff hear about timetable changes from pupils before they hear from leaders" |
| "Ease of use" | "New users give up at the permissions step because they cannot tell what is being asked of them" |
| "Mixed views on training" | "Early-career staff value the training; experienced staff find it repeats what they know" |

## Counting

Give counts with denominators, by group where that matters: "9 of 14 participants; 6 of
the 7 new starters".

A count describes the people asked. It shows a pattern exists and how far it ran among
them. It does not estimate how common the pattern is in the wider population unless the
sample was drawn for that purpose. Do not turn small counts into percentages: "64%" from
9 of 14 claims a precision the data do not have.

Frequency is not importance. A problem two people raised can outweigh one twelve people
raised if the consequence is severe. Say which of the two is driving the judgement.

## Accounts and actions

What people say they do, want or would do is weaker than what they did.

- Prefer accounts of particular past events to generalisations and forecasts.
- Record stated preferences and intentions as exactly that.
- Where behaviour is also recorded, set it beside the account. A gap between them is a
  finding, often the most useful one.

Keep description and inference in separate claims. "Seven participants described
exporting the data to a spreadsheet" is a `finding`. "The reporting screen does not
meet their needs" is an `interpretation` based on it.

## The cases that do not fit

For every theme, record the participants who contradict it under `contradicts`, and say
in `reconciled` what distinguishes them. Usually this sharpens the claim into a
conditional one ("except where a mentor was assigned"), which is more useful than the
unconditional version. If nothing distinguishes them, write `unexplained` and let the
ceiling fall.

## What the data cannot tell you

Say so in the report, and say what would settle it, when:

- a theme rests on two or three people and the decision needs more than that;
- a group that matters to the decision was not spoken to, or barely;
- two themes conflict with no visible reason;
- later interviews were still raising things earlier ones had not;
- the questions asked could not have brought the matter up;
- everyone asked had a reason to answer one way (volunteers, the satisfied, the aggrieved).

The pull to conclude is strongest once a tidy account has formed. Check it against this
list before writing.

## Quotations and privacy

- A quotation shows what a theme sounds like; it is not the analysis. One or two per
  theme, after the claim, each with a participant id and locator.
- Keep them short and exact.
- Use neutral ids and describe people by the attributes that matter. In a small group,
  check that the combination of attributes does not point to one person.
- Remove names and identifying detail unless the user confirms attribution was agreed.
- Leave out sensitive personal detail the finding does not need.

## Credibility checks

Suggest those the user is in a position to arrange, and report which were done:

- **Participant validation**: do the people who took part recognise the account?
- **Peer debrief**: does someone who knows the setting but did not do the analysis find
  it credible, and what do they think is missing?
- **Triangulation**: is there behavioural or quantitative data that could confirm or
  contradict the main themes?
- **Negative case review**: has someone tried to argue each theme is wrong?
