## Turning a request into an answerable question

Most requests arrive as a topic ("what do we know about retrieval practice?"). A topic
cannot be answered; a question can. Find the question by asking what decision or purpose
the synthesis serves:

- "Should we adopt X?" needs effectiveness, cost, conditions and risks.
- "Why is Y happening?" needs competing explanations and the evidence for each.
- "What is known about Z?" is a mapping question; use a scoping approach and do not
  pretend to estimate effects.
- "How do people experience W?" is a qualitative question; counting is secondary.

Write the question so that an answer could be "no" or "it depends on...". If two
questions are tangled together, separate them and synthesise each.

## Question frameworks

Use a framework only to check that nothing essential is missing from the question.

| Framework | Elements | Suits |
| --- | --- | --- |
| PICO | Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcome | Effectiveness of an intervention |
| PECO | Population, Exposure, Comparison, Outcome | Risk factors and associations |
| PCC | Population, Concept, Context | Scoping and mapping questions |
| SPIDER | Sample, Phenomenon of Interest, Design, Evaluation, Research type | Qualitative and mixed-methods questions |
| SPICE | Setting, Perspective, Intervention, Comparison, Evaluation | Service and policy evaluation |

For non-academic questions (markets, technology, organisational practice) plain language
is fine: who or what, doing what, compared with what, judged by what, in which context
and over what period.

## Choosing a review type

| Type | Purpose | Search | Appraisal | Synthesis |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Rapid review | Timely answer for a decision | Focused, limits stated | Abbreviated | Narrative, structured |
| Scoping review | Map what exists and where the gaps are | Broad | Usually none or light | Descriptive mapping, tables |
| Systematic review | Answer a focused question reproducibly | Exhaustive | Formal, per study | Narrative, quantitative or qualitative |
| Umbrella review | Synthesise existing reviews | Reviews only | Review-level tool | Compare review conclusions |
| Realist review | What works, for whom, in what circumstances, why | Iterative, purposive | Relevance and rigour | Context-mechanism-outcome patterns |
| Qualitative evidence synthesis | Understand experience and meaning | Purposive or exhaustive | Qualitative checklist | Thematic, framework or meta-ethnography |
| Mixed-methods review | Effect plus experience or implementation | Both strands | Per design | Separate strands, then integrate |

Mapping to this skill's modes: rapid review is Rapid or Standard; scoping, umbrella,
realist and qualitative syntheses are usually Standard; a systematic review is Systematic.

Be honest about the label. A synthesis that searched two databases in an afternoon is a
rapid review, not a systematic review, however carefully it was written. Naming the type
accurately tells the reader how much weight the result will bear.

If good recent systematic reviews already exist on the question, start from them (an
umbrella approach) and add newer primary studies, instead of redoing their work.

## Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Set each of these before reading the evidence:

- **Population or context**: who or what the evidence must be about, and how far
  neighbouring populations count as indirect evidence.
- **Intervention, exposure or phenomenon**: with a working definition, since terms vary
  between fields.
- **Comparison**: against what.
- **Outcomes**: which matter to the decision, and which measures count.
- **Study designs and source types**: what is admissible, including grey literature.
- **Time window**: with a reason (a policy change, a technology shift, an earlier review).
- **Language and geography**: with the limitation stated.
- **Minimum quality**: whether very weak sources are excluded or included and
  down-weighted. Including and down-weighting is usually better, since exclusion hides
  them from the reader.

Criteria should be specific enough that two people would make the same decision about
the same source.

## Protocol template

Record this at the start. In the ledger it lives under `protocol`.

```
Question:
Decision or purpose served:
Audience:
Mode (rapid / standard / systematic) and review type:
Corpus (supplied / searched / mixed):
Inclusion criteria:
Exclusion criteria:
Sources to search (databases, registers, sites, people):
Appraisal approach:
Synthesis method (planned):
Known constraints (time, access, languages):
Assumptions made without confirmation:
```

For Systematic mode, write the protocol before searching and report any deviation from
it. Follow the PRISMA 2020 reporting items for the final report, and PRISMA-ScR for a
scoping review.

## Search log and flow counts

Log every search as it is run:

| Where | Query | Date | Results |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| e.g. database or site | exact string, including operators and filters | YYYY-MM-DD | n |

Track counts at each stage so the report can state them:

```
Records identified -> duplicates removed -> screened on title and abstract
-> full texts assessed -> included
```

Record the reason for each full-text exclusion. Count records, reports and studies
separately: one study may produce several reports, and it should enter the synthesis once.
