# Would flexible start times across all 30 depots reduce staff turnover?

*Evidence gathered to: 12 November 2026. Type: rapid synthesis of supplied documents. Sources
included: 4 of 5 supplied (3 read in full, 1 available only as an abstract).*

*Assumptions made without confirmation: "turnover" means voluntary leavers as a share of
staff in post; the five supplied documents are everything available, and no search was run.*

## Bottom line

Flexible start times may reduce voluntary turnover by a few percentage points in the
first six to twelve months, but the only evidence that looks beyond a year found no
lasting difference. That is not enough to justify a roll-out to all 30 depots. It is
enough to justify a staged trial that would settle the question.

## What this means for the decision

- **Recommendation** [C6]: do not roll out to all 30 depots on this evidence. Extend
  flexible starts to a randomly chosen subset of depots and track payroll turnover for at
  least 18 months before deciding. (Certainty: Low. A possible short-term benefit of
  uncertain durability.)
- **Interpretation** [C5]: if the early reduction fades, one possible reason is that
  popular start slots fill up and newer staff are left with little real choice [S5].
  (Certainty: Very low. Managers' accounts in one study, not tested.) If a trial goes
  ahead, record which slots new starters actually get.
- **What would change this**: a lasting difference at 18 months in a randomised
  comparison would support roll-out; no difference would end the case for it on
  retention grounds.

## Findings

### 1. Flexible start times may reduce turnover by a few points in the first six to twelve months [C1]

Certainty: Low. Two independent studies [S1, S5] agree in direction, but both intervals reach
zero and each study has a design weakness.

In a pilot that randomly assigned 12 depots, twelve-month voluntary turnover was 13.0%
(78 of 602 staff) with flexible starts against 17.0% (99 of 582) with fixed starts, a
difference of 4.0 percentage points (95% CI 0.0 to 8.1) [S1]. The analysis did not adjust
for clustering by depot, so the true interval is wider than reported, and two of the six
flexible depots opened canteens during the pilot [S1]. An independent evaluation of 9
flexible and 9 matched depots found turnover of 6.1% against 8.4% in months 1 to 6, a
difference of -2.3 points (95% CI -4.6 to 0.0); depots were matched, not randomised [S5].
A vendor's case study reports turnover falling from 25% to 15% in a year at one employer,
but pay rose 9% and the night shift closed in the same year, so the fall cannot be
attributed to flexible starts [S4].

### 2. The reduction may not last beyond the first year [C2]

Certainty: Low. A single non-randomised study with a wide interval.

From month 7 to month 18 the independent evaluation found turnover of 14.9% in flexible
depots against 14.2% in matched depots, a difference of +0.7 points (95% CI -2.4 to 3.8)
[S5]. The pilot's twelve-month result does not contradict this: it reports cumulative
turnover and does not separate early from late months [S1].

### 3. It is unclear whether any benefit is concentrated among newer staff [C3]

Certainty: Very low. One unplanned subgroup analysis, in tension with the other study.

In the pilot, turnover among staff with under a year's tenure was 21.4% with flexible
starts against 31.9% with fixed starts, while for longer-serving staff it was 9.8%
against 10.6%; this analysis was not planned in advance [S1]. Managers at five of the
nine flexible depots in the independent evaluation said that newer staff were usually
left with the least popular slots [S5]. The two observations pull in different
directions and neither study tests the question.

### 4. Staff with control over their start time are less likely to say they intend to leave [C4]

Certainty: Low. One cross-sectional survey, read only as an abstract.

A survey of 2,310 staff across 41 employers found lower odds of intending to leave
within a year among those with some control over start time (odds ratio 0.71, 95% CI
0.58 to 0.87), with a 22% response rate [S3]. This is an association with stated
intention. It does not show that offering flexibility causes fewer people to leave.

## Where the evidence disagrees

The pilot and the evaluation agree for the early months and appear to differ after
that [S1, S5]. The likeliest reason is the time horizon: the pilot stops at twelve
months and reports one cumulative figure. On who benefits, the two studies point in
different directions and the conflict is unexplained [S1, S5].

The trade press article supplied describes the pilot as cutting turnover "by a quarter"
and as proof. It is coverage of the pilot, not separate evidence, and was excluded: the
"quarter" is the same 4-point difference expressed as a relative change.

## Gaps and limitations

**Gaps in the evidence**: no randomised evidence beyond twelve months; no evidence on
cost, cover or operational effects; nothing from this organisation's own depots.

**Limits of this synthesis**: it covers only the five documents supplied; one source was
available only as an abstract; one reviewer; no search for further studies.

## How this was done

- **Question**: would flexible start times across all 30 depots reduce voluntary turnover?
- **Criteria**: sources reporting turnover, or intention to leave, with and without
  flexible starts in comparable shift-based work; coverage that adds no data excluded.
- **Search**: none; limited to the documents supplied.
- **Screening**: 5 supplied, 1 excluded as coverage of another, 4 included.
- **Appraisal**: design-appropriate questions per source; each source assigned a lineage
  so that repeated reports of one study count once.
- **Synthesis method**: narrative synthesis with a structured summary of effects.
